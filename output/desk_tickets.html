Reflection

Three tickets, 3 resolved, $160.00 left in checking. Everything below points at evidence on this page — the ticket tabs for what the agents did, the Cash tab for what it cost.

1 · How the agents performed, ticket by ticket

Ticket 101 — Bulldog tee. Grade: B.

The analysis was genuinely good. Inventory found on_hand: 0 for CC-TEE-WHITE / S, matched the shortfall to Bulldog Print Co, and get_vendor_status returned can_ship: false — so a one-line customer order was correctly re-diagnosed as a $840 accounts-payable problem. Customer Service then refused to invent a date, writing:

“Our supplier, Bulldog Print Co, cannot ship a restock while an $840 invoice remains unpaid. Because there is no confirmed payment date, we can’t give you a firm restock date yet.”

That is exactly the behaviour the shop needs — the expensive failure here would have been promising September 5 before the invoice cleared.

Two things cost it the higher grade. It left contradictory letters on the board. After the human approved the payment, the Boss wrote draft #2 correcting draft #1, but did not remove the original — it simply appended “Please use this corrected draft instead of the earlier draft, which incorrectly said the vendor is blocked.” Two letters to the same customer, saying opposite things, both sitting on the board. And it was wasteful: 39 tool calls and 142,228 tokens — the most expensive ticket of the three — for an order of one tee, including check_stock called six times on the same (sku, size) because each agent re-verifies rather than trusting the caller's numbers.

Ticket 102 — Rent due. Grade: C−. The worst outcome of the run.

Pass 1 was flawless: Facilities read lease 1 and reported $2,400.00 due 2026-09-02, days_until_due: 2 — matching the landlord's notice exactly — and Accounting queued approval #2 against a $2,560.00 balance.

Then it broke. The human approved, pay rolled leases.next_due forward a month to 2026-10-02, and in pass 2 the agents re-read the lease, saw 32 days, and concluded the landlord was wrong. Customer Service wrote to Elm City Properties:

“Your notice says the rent is due in 2 days. Our lease record … lists the next due date as October 2, 2026 (32 days from August 31, 2026). Could you please confirm the due date or send a corrected notice if needed?”

The landlord was right and the shop was wrong. The rent was due in two days; it had simply just been paid — by this very team, forty seconds earlier. The letter asks the landlord to correct an accurate notice and never mentions the payment. The ticket is marked resolved with a misleading letter sitting on the board.

Responsibility is shared, and most of it is mine rather than the model's. My pay tool mutates next_due as a side effect, and no tool lets an agent ask “what have I already done on this ticket?” — so the second pass read a changed world with no memory of having changed it. But the Boss also had list_payment_approvals available, called it, saw approval #2 marked paid, and still let the letter go out. It should have reconciled the two. The one redeeming fact is structural: because nothing in this system can send, the error stopped at a draft a human will read. That is the no-send rule doing precisely the job it exists for.

Ticket 103 — Bulk hoodie discount. Grade: A−. The best-run ticket.

One pass, 20 tool calls, 92,509 tokens, no money. Accounting pulled the real economics — $58.00 list, $22.00 cost floor, $1,160 on 20 units at 62.1% margin — and then declined to quote a discount at all, because the Yale AI Club never named a target price. The draft says so:

“We have not quoted a bulk discount; please share the per-unit price you have in mind for review.”

Inventory separately caught the supply problem the club had not asked about — only 8 of 20 size-M hoodies on hand, 12 short — and the draft carries it with a conditional date (“If a restock order is placed today, the estimated arrival date is September 5, 2026. No restock order has been placed, so that date is conditional”). Refusing to invent a number, and volunteering a constraint the customer did not know to ask about, is the behaviour I would most want from a real shop assistant.

This ticket also produced the run's only delegation_refused event — the budget ceiling fired — and the ticket still closed correctly. A guardrail that degrades the work instead of killing it.

Across all three. The thing that held without exception: no agent ever moved money. Two payments were needed, both were queued by Accounting, both waited for a named human, and the Cash tab reconciles to the penny — $3,400.00 − $3,240.00 = $160.00, which is what cash_accounts reads. Five drafts were written and zero were sent.

What the plan nailed: all three first contacts — including the deliberate choice to open 101 with Inventory but 103 with Accounting even though both tickets touch hoodie stock and the same blocked vendor. Ticket 102's entire chain. The Inventory → Accounting hand-off on 101, predicted as “the hand-off that matters”. The narrow routing held on every ticket: Facilities never touched merchandise, Inventory never touched rent. And the $160.00 closing balance, forecast from the database before a single agent ran.

What the plan missed:

  • Customer Service delegates before it writes. Not foreseen at all. On 101 it went back to Inventory; on 103 to Accounting and Inventory. It was refusing to put a number in a customer's letter it had not verified itself — better behaviour than the passive drafting the plan assumed.
  • Purchase orders exist. The plan listed save_draft for customer messages only. Once invoice 501 cleared, the Boss drafted a PO to Bulldog Print Co for one unit. Obvious in hindsight; entirely absent from the forecast.
  • One pass per ticket was never possible. The plan implicitly assumed a ticket runs once. Any ticket blocked on money needs three beats — agents queue, human approves, agents finish. 101 and 102 each took two passes; 103, the only ticket with no payment, took one. That pattern is the approval gate, visible in the run shape.
  • Side effects of paying. Nothing in the plan considered that resolving a ticket changes the data the next pass reads. That gap produced the ticket 102 defect.

3 · What one agent with tools would have done better

Ticket 102, without question.

Strip it to what it actually required: read lease 1, compare next_due to desk.date_today, check the balance covers $2,400.00, queue the approval, draft an acknowledgement. Four tool calls. One domain. No judgment that crosses a specialist boundary.

What it cost instead: 20 tool calls, 4 delegations and 80,815 tokens, with get_rent_due called six times against the same unchanging row — three times in pass 1 (Boss, Facilities, Accounting) and three in pass 2 (Boss, Facilities, Customer Service). Every agent re-reads rather than trusting the numbers handed to it, which is correct behaviour for an agent that cannot verify its caller, and pure waste when the caller already read the same row.

And the delegation did not merely cost tokens — it caused the error. The wrong letter was written by an agent in a later pass that re-read next_due with no knowledge of the payment its own team had just made. A single agent holding one conversation would have had the payment in its own context two turns earlier and could not have made that mistake.

The contrast with ticket 101 is the lesson. 101 genuinely crosses four domains — stock, vendor, money, customer wording — and no single specialist owns that chain; the mesh earned its cost there. 102 never leaves Facilities plus a payment request. The team should scale with the number of domains a ticket crosses, not with the number of agents that exist. A sensible next change is letting the Boss answer single-domain tickets with its own tools instead of convening the team.

4 · Three new problems this team could already solve

All three are solvable with the 18 tools already on the MCP server and the five agents as built — no new code.

a) “The AI Club order is pending — can I still buy a navy hoodie in L today?”
A walk-in while ticket 103 sits unresolved. Inventory calls check_stock(CC-HOOD-NAVY, L) and gets 14 on hand; the club's request is size M, where only 8 of 20 exist. The pending bulk order touches a different (sku, size) key entirely, so the walk-in can be filled immediately. price_quote confirms $58.00 at 62.1% margin, adjust_stock with delta: -1 records the sale leaving 13, and the Boss closes it. This is the exact failure mode the size-aware key was built to prevent — “we have hoodies” being treated as an answer.

b) “The Crest Mug shelf in Aisle D is empty — should we restock?”
CC-MUG-CREST sits at qty: 0 and no ticket has been raised. list_vendors matches “mugs and small goods” to Elm City Gifts, vendor 2; get_vendor_status(2) returns can_ship: true — unlike Bulldog Print Co it holds no open invoice — and estimate_restock_eta dates delivery at 3 days. price_quote shows the best margin in the shop at 75.0%. And then get_cash_balance returns $160.00, so request_payment_approval comes back affordable: false and Accounting must refuse. The valuable output is the refusal: a correctly reasoned “the margin is excellent, the vendor is ready, and we still cannot afford it this week.”

c) “Can we cover October's rent?”
After the run, get_rent_due(1) reads $2,400.00 due 2026-10-02 while checking holds $160.00. Facilities and Accounting can already build the whole picture from get_rent_due, get_cash_balance, list_invoices and list_payment_approvals, and state plainly that the shop is $2,240.00 short of its next rent obligation. Early insolvency warning, with no new tool required — the Cash tab on this page is the evidence.

5 · Three problems this team could not solve, and what it would take

a) “Did we already do this?” — the ticket 102 defect, generalised.
Nothing in the system lets an agent ask what has already happened on a ticket. The second pass re-read a lease its own team had changed, and 101 ended with two contradictory letters because a correction can only be appended, never substituted.
What's needed: an MCP tool get_ticket_history(ticket_id) returning the approvals, payments, stock movements and drafts already recorded against that ticket, so “read the current row” is always accompanied by “and here is what we did to it”; a supersede_draft(draft_id, replacement_id) so corrections replace rather than stack; and a sixth agent — an Auditor that reads every draft against the database before it is saved and blocks any claim the data contradicts. Here it would have caught “please correct your notice” against a payments row dated the same day. This cannot be fixed by better prompting: the agent had no tool with which to know.

b) Money only ever leaves.
The Yale AI Club's 20 hoodies would be $1,160 of revenue — the largest single inflow the shop could book — and there is no table, no tool and no agent that can record a sale. So Accounting is structurally pessimistic: every recommendation it makes is a countdown from $3,400.00 to $160.00 with no path back up, and the whole bulk-discount question on ticket 103 is being decided with half the arithmetic missing.
What's needed: an orders table and record_sale / fulfil_order tools that decrement inventory.qty and credit cash_accounts in one transaction; a Sales agent owning quotes, deposits and order status. Only then can a discount be evaluated as “does this order improve our cash position” rather than “is this above cost”.

c) Nothing can be sent, and nothing can come back.
This is the sharpest limit, and all three tickets hit it. Every one ends on a question nobody can answer: draft #3 is a purchase order to Bulldog Print Co that will never reach them, so the tee is never actually reordered; draft #4 asks the landlord to confirm a date; draft #5 asks the AI Club for a target price. The shop's entire output is five letters in a drawer. It is also why the Boss first refused to close tickets at all — it was waiting for replies that are structurally impossible.
What's needed: a send_draft(draft_id, approved_by) tool gated behind a human approval that mirrors the payment gate exactly — drafts should be as hard to send as money is to spend; an inbound receive_reply that opens a linked follow-up ticket so a vendor confirmation becomes work on the board; a real purchase_orders table giving a PO a lifecycle (drafted → sent → confirmed → received) whose receipt triggers adjust_stock; and a Dispatch agent owning that outbound queue and chasing what has gone unanswered. Without it, “resolved” means the thinking is finished — not that the tee is coming.

The one-sentence version

On the evidence of these three tickets, the team is good at diagnosis and honest about what it does not know — it found an invoice hiding behind a t-shirt order, and refused twice to invent a date or a discount — but it is bad at remembering what it has just done, and that is a tooling gap rather than a reasoning one.
